3.1.1 In-detail - the Aristotle’s Hylomorphism

Per the subject topic, of which more philosophical than not, in Ainsworth (2016) it was to be said that Aristotle’s hylomorphism is rather advanced for its time. The philosophical doctrine of such posits that all physical entities are compounds of matter (hyle - the material constituent or potentiality) and form (morphe or eidos - the structure essence or actuality), to explain of the material and the world.

The notion is toward what are essentially compositional thought, for Aristotle of his general ideas. Aristotle defines matter as to be ‘out of which’ something is made. For example, letters are the matter of syllables. Thus, matter is a relative term: an object counts as matter relative to something else. Clay is matter relative to a brick because a brick is made of clay, at that specific point, whereas bricks are matter relative to a brick house. Change is analyzed as a material transformation, for where matter is what undergoes a change of form. In all, it is also the relational composition that marks the strength of this approach, and perhaps even now this line of thought has only been refined, much not disappeared entirely, because of the act it allows that would be central to pretty much the way down toward atomic scale, and so on.

## Pre-material science

The history has not been so kind to the precursors of material science on itself. The definition has changed rapidly, repeatedly, each change reflecting a different commitment, not realization, not unification or reverse calibration, but rather to about what kinds of things exist and what kinds of relations between things are scientifically fundamental, if such is even possible thereof. 

History of pre-material science can be separated into the concept word of **matter**. ### In-detail - the Aristotle's Hylomorphism

The notion is toward what are essentially **compositional thought**, for Aristotle of his general ideas. Aristotle defines matter as to be 'out of which' something is made. ### The Thomson Atomic Model

Per [@thomson1904structure], [@thomson1907corpuscular], [@thomson1897cathode], [@thomson1903magnetic], [@thomson1904electricity], [@hon2013myth] and [@renstrom2022textbook], J.J. Generally speaking before going in any further details, the setup, despite its myriad of different results and motivation that led to such, is rather simple, and quite interesting. For the lack of a better description and of its weirdness,

> Scientists had now established that the atom was not indivisible as Dalton had believed, and due to the work of Thomson, Millikan, and others, the charge and mass of the negative, subatomic particles—the electrons—were known. However, the positively charged part of an atom was not yet well understood. In 1904, Thomson proposed the **“plum pudding”** model of atoms, which described a positively charged mass with an equal amount of negative charge in the form of electrons embedded in it, since all atoms are electrically neutral. A competing model had been proposed in 1903 by *Hantaro Nagaoka*, who postulated a Saturn-like atom, consisting of a positively charged sphere surrounded by a **halo** of electrons.

A full historical account can be seen, of [@baily2008atomic] on the matter of details. That said, much can be said about how Thomson model was realized and what is its structure in more details. In 1897, Joseph John Thomson discovered a corpuscle, much later called *electron*, of which are *negatively charged*. The nature of it being negatively charge, and per convention with its contingent of positively charged atoms, gave rise to the idea of targeting the specificity in chemical periodicity and composition, using those newly seen charge. As said of [@hentschel2009plum], Thomson's idea is rather natural. Consider that an atom is electronically neutral, then it must be so that the total electric charge of all "these negatively charged electrons had to be compensated for by an equal amount of positive charge". 

The problem now arises to say, how would those charges to be distributed of. Here, $C$ is a correction potential factor. However, this form is rather suspicious. Why it would have to be this of the atom to get this definite electronic architecture? #### The Thomson's paper - *On the Structure of the Atom*

Let us trace his paper from the start. It begins as to state of the central problem of the time - the view that "the atoms of elements consist of a number of **negatively electrified** (charged) corpuscles enclosed in a sphere of uniform **positive electrification**", of which suggest a configuration where $n$ negatively electrified, or so *electron*, corpuscles are probed of their motions inside such system. What he was hinting at, is toward the *existence of particles*, thus posit that the configuration of the system from his knowledge and of the time, to be in equilibrium. Such is why the problem starts with equilibrium structure situation, and continue on with motions inside this equilibrium sphere. We note that under this view, none of the $e_{n}$ escapes the structure of the atom itself. 

For charge $ve$ of the sphere, $b$ sphere radius and $a$ radius of the corpuscle, then
$$
G_{corp}= \frac{ve^{2}a}{b^{3}}, \quad G_{ij} = \frac{e^{2}}{4r_{i}^{2}\sin{(1/2)\theta_{ij}}}
$$
is the positive electrification force attracting the corpuscle; here, $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle made by the joint $r_{i}$ and $r_{j}$, of two objects $A$ and $B$ of such distance from the centre of the sphere. This presupposes *radially uniform, single directed* attractive force toward the corpuscle, even inside the positive sphere. Surprisingly, yet rather interesting, this also conditions a point-attractive **valley**, or an atom. The other forces in action, is the repulsion $G_{ij}$, of which for uniform distribution of $2\pi/n$ on a single ring, then the radial repulsion on one corpuscle due to the other $(n-1)$ is equal to
$$
\frac{e^{2}}{4a^{2}} \left( \csc{\frac{\pi}{n}} + \csc{\frac{2\pi}{n}} + \csc{\frac{3\pi}{n}} + \dots + \csc{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{n}} \right)
$$
The radial attraction, $G_{corp}=S_{n}$ for $n$ corpuscles, in equilibrium position, must equal to such expression, hence, 
$$
S_{n}= \frac{e^{2}}{4a^{2}} \left( \csc{\frac{\pi}{n}} + \csc{\frac{2\pi}{n}} + \csc{\frac{3\pi}{n}} + \dots + \csc{\frac{(n-1)\pi}{n}} \right) \equiv \frac{e^{2}}{4a^{2}} \sum^{n}_{i=2}\csc{\frac{(i-1)\pi}{i}} 
$$
or, in a simplified form, 
$$
\frac{a^{3}}{b^{3}} = \frac{S_{n}}{4v}
$$
The separated values are computed explicitly with both $S_{n}$ and $a/b$, using empirical predictions of the time. If the ring of the corpuscles, instead of being at rest, is rotating with an angular velocity $\omega$, the condition for steady motion is
$$
\frac{ve^{2}a}{b^{3}} = ma\omega^{2} + \frac{e^{2}}{4a^{2}} S_{n}
$$
or with manipulation algebraically, 
$$
\frac{va^{3}}{b^{3}} = \frac{m}{e^{2}}\omega^{2} + \frac{S_{n}}{4}
$$
here $m$ is the mass of a corpuscle. In the subsequent phases, after dealing with equilibrium state, what would happen is the argument when the corpuscles are slightly displaced from their positions equilibrium. Let the position of the corpuscles be fixed by the polar coordinates $r$ and $\theta$ in the plane of the undisturbed orbit, by displacement $z$ at right angles to this plane. Let $r_{s},\theta_{s},z_{s}$ be the coordinates of the $s$-th corpuscle. The slight disturbance from their positions can be said to be $r_{s}=a+\rho_{s}$ where $\rho_{s}$is small compared with $a$, $z_{s}$ is also small compared with $a$, and $\theta_{s}-\theta_{s-1}=2\pi/n + \phi_{s}-\phi_{s-1}$, where $n$ is the number of corpuscles and $\phi$'s are small quantities of the same form on perturbation mechanics. The intention is to see if the condition can be reverted of equilibrium from the radial repulsion, which encapsulates the system by description to stabilize the atom. For such, the radial repulsion exerted by the $s$-th corpuscle on the $p$-th is equal to 
$$
-e^{2} \frac{d}{dr_{p}} \frac{1}{(r^{2}_{p}+r_{s}^{2}-2r_{p}r_{s}\cos{(\theta_{s}-\theta_{p})}+(z_{p}-z_{s})^{2})^{1/2}}
$$
For this, solving and deriving, he obtains the equation of motion of the $p$-th corpuscle under that constrained and perturbation, given as
$$
\begin{align*}
    & m \left(\frac{d^{2}r_{p}}{dt^{2}}-r_{p} \left(\frac{d\theta_{p}}{dt}\right)^{2} \right) = -\frac{ve^{2}r_{p}}{b^{3}}+ R_{p}; \\
    & m \left( r \frac{d^{2}\theta_{p}}{dt^{2}} + 2\frac{dr}{dt} \frac{d\theta_{p}}{dt} \right) = \Theta_{p} \\
    & m \frac{d^{2}z_{p}}{dt^{2}} = -\frac{ve^{2}}{b^{3}}z_{p} + Z_{p}
\end{align*}
$$
for individual radial, tangential and right angle forces, respectively. Solving this using frequency theory of the time, he obtains the frequency equation as 
$$
\left( \frac{3}{4} \frac{e^{2}}{a^{3}} S + L_{k} - L_{0} - mq^{2} \right) (N_{0}-N_{k}-mq^{2}) = (M_{k} - 2m\omega q)^{2}
$$
for $k$ in this equation varies of order from $0$ to $(n-1)$. The notation is defined in the document, of which would be rather interested if you are to see from the paper itself (a copy can be found [here](https://uni-tuebingen.de/fileadmin/Uni_Tuebingen/Fakultaeten/MathePhysik/Institute/IAP/Forschung/MOettel/Geburt_QM/thomson_PhilMag_7_237_1904.pdf), respectively). By a series of derivation, solving and targeting the right angles motion, the problem is reduced to the expression for the descriptive dynamics as
$$
\frac{ve}{b^3} + P_{k} - P_{0} - mq^{2} = 0
$$ 
Putting in succession $k=0,1,...,n-1$, we can get $n$ values of $q$ giving the $n$ frequencies corresponding to the displacements at right angles to the plane of the undisturbed orbit. To then calculate the frequencies for systems containing various numbers of corpuscles, the four quantities specified above, namely $L_k, M_k, N_k, P_k$ which occurs in the frequency equation, can now be completely computed by
$$
\begin{align*}
    T_{k} & = \sum^{n-1}_{1}\cos{\frac{2\pi ks}{n}} \left( \sin^{3}{\frac{s\pi}{n}}\right)^{-1} \\
    S_{k} & = \sum^{n-1}_{1}\cos{\frac{2\pi ks}{n}} \left( \sin{\frac{s\pi}{n}}\right)^{-1} \\
    U_{k} & = \sum^{n-1}_{1}\sin{\frac{2\pi ks}{n}} \left( \dfrac{\cos{\dfrac{s\pi}{n}}}{\sin^{2}{\dfrac{s\pi}{n}}}\right)
\end{align*}
$$
which gives
$$
\begin{matrix}
    L_{k} (S_{k}+T_{k}) \dfrac{e^{2}}{8a^{3}} & N_{k} = (2T_{k} - S_{k}) \dfrac{e^{2}}{8a^{3}} \\
    M_{k} = U_{k} \dfrac{e^{2}}{8a^{3}} & P_{k} = T_{k} \dfrac{e^{2}}{8a^{3}}
\end{matrix}
$$
This can be then used, in the paper, to solve for case of two corpuscles, i.e. $n=2$ up to $n=6$. Interestingly, the model predicts instability at $n\geq 7$ and so on, particularly requiring stability analysis of sort. Indeed, "for a single corpuscle in the centre is sufficient to make rings of 7 and 8 corpuscles stable", thence given such, there exists an extreme minimal on which a single electron repulse and operate on the dual purpose - both being electronic configuration of the system, both providing repulsive stabilisation force. When the number of corpuscles exceeds 8, however, "the number of central corpuscles required to ensure stability *increases very rapidly* with the number of corpuscles in the ring". The frequency equation, that is, 
$$
\left(\frac{3 e^{2} S_{0}}{4 a^{3}}+ \frac{3 p e^{2}}{a^{3}}- \left(L_{0} - L_{k}\right)- m q^{2}\right)\left(N_{0} - N_{k} - m q^{2}\right)=\left(M_{k} - 2 m \omega q\right)^{2}.
$$
gives the least condition, that is, 
$$
\frac{3pe^{2}}{a^{3}} > (L_{0}-L_{n/2}) - \frac{3}{4} \frac{e^{2}S_{0}}{a^{3}}, \quad \frac{3pe^{2}}{a^{3}} > (L_{0}-L_{(n-1)/2}) - \frac{3}{4} \frac{e^{2}S_{0}}{a^{3}}
$$
Here, $p$ is the number of stabilization electrons, or as stated of the paper, **internal corpuscles**. Indeed, within such, we can see the pattern as shown in the calculation of Thomson:
